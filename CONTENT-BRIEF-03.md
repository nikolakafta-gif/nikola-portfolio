## Article Outline

### H1: How Long Does It Take to Build a Website?

### Answer-first intro (no heading, 90–120 words)
Numbers in the first two sentences: landing page 3 to 7 days, standard 3 to 5 page business site 1 to 3 weeks, content-rich 6 to 10 page site 2 to 4 weeks, e-commerce or custom functionality 4 to 8+ weeks. Then the qualifier that carries the whole article: those are build times once content is ready, and content readiness is what decides whether a two-week build lands in two weeks or two months.

### H2: How Long Does It Take to Build a Website?
The extractable answer block. Ranges by site type as grouped bullets (no tables, see format note), each with what is included at that size. Close with the sentence AI engines can lift: the build is the predictable part, the client-side inputs are not.

### H2: What Do Real Website Timelines Look Like?
The differentiator. Eight live projects with actual durations, each linked:
- Comira, fitness landing page, 3 days
- Porta Brzi Brod, bilingual restaurant site, 4 days
- Doorly, real estate landing page, 1 week
- Victoria, coaching site, 1 week
- Casera, hotel site, 2 weeks
- Advisia, consulting site, 2 weeks
- Vistal, architecture firm redesign, 2 weeks
- Zvonačka Banja, tourism site, 2 weeks
Then the pattern reading: nothing here took months, and the 3-day project was not the simplest one, it was the one where the client had content ready and made decisions quickly.

### H2: What Happens in Each Stage of the Build?
Stage breakdown with day ranges for a standard business site (1 to 3 weeks total):
- Brief and scope, 1 to 2 days
- Design or wireframe, 2 to 4 days
- Build, 3 to 7 days
- Review and revisions, 2 to 4 days (client-paced)
- Testing and launch, 1 to 2 days
Include the pre-launch performance check here, with the LCP citation.

### H2: Why Do Most Website Projects Take Longer Than Planned?
The honest section. Ranked by how often it actually happens:
1. Content is not ready (the single biggest cause)
2. Slow feedback loops (a 3-day reply on every round adds a week to a two-week build)
3. Scope growing mid-build
4. Too many decision makers
5. Third-party dependencies (payment providers, booking systems, domain access)

### H2: Why Do Agencies Quote 2 to 3 Months for the Same Website?
Answers the contradiction the reader will hit on every other page one result, where 8 to 16 weeks is normal. Explain honestly: discovery phases, approval chains, queue position, and the fact that a bigger scope genuinely takes longer. Not a dig, a scoping difference. Link to the freelance vs agency comparison.

### H2: What Can You Do to Make Your Website Launch Faster?
Actionable checklist: write the copy before the build starts, gather images at full resolution, name one decision maker, book review slots in the calendar in advance, get domain and hosting access sorted early, keep phase 2 as phase 2.

### H2: How Long After Launch Before Your Site Shows Up on Google?
The section almost nobody covers, and it is where the reader's real deadline usually sits. Days to weeks for crawling, longer for ranking, cite Google Search Central. Explain what to do at launch: sitemap in Search Console, request indexing on priority URLs, and that ranking is a separate multi-month track.

### H2: Frequently Asked Questions
8 questions, 40–60 words each, self-contained.

### Closing (no "Conclusion" heading, match existing posts)
One short paragraph restating the ranges, then the CTA to `/contact` framed around dates: tell me the date you need it live and I will tell you whether it is realistic.

## Competitive Notes

Page one is hosting companies and site builders (Hostinger, Bluehost, Namecheap, Elementor, Network Solutions) plus agencies. Typical length 1,500–3,000 words. Two consistent patterns:

- The builders answer with their own product in mind, so the range starts at "a weekend" and the article becomes a funnel to a sign-up.
- The agencies answer 8 to 16 weeks, sometimes 9 months, because they are describing enterprise scope with discovery phases.

What they all cover: ranges by site type, factors affecting timeline, stage breakdown.
What none of them provide: named live projects with their actual durations, or the post-launch indexing wait.

## Differentiation

1. **Eight real projects with real durations**, each one a live site the reader can open. Every competitor uses hypothetical ranges.
2. **Reconciles the contradiction** between "one weekend" and "three months" instead of ignoring it, by separating build time from scope and from client-side delay.
3. **Content readiness treated as the actual variable**, with a concrete example: the 3-day project was fast because the content was written before the build started.
4. **Includes the post-launch indexing wait**, which is the reader's real question when they have a launch date.
