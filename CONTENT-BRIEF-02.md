## Article Outline

### H1: Freelance Developer vs Agency: Which Should You Hire?

### Answer-first intro (no heading, 90–120 words)
Direct answer in the first two sentences: for a standard website — a landing page up to about 10 pages — a freelance developer is usually the better choice on cost, speed and direct communication. An agency earns its premium when the project spans several disciplines at once, needs guaranteed availability during business hours, or carries compliance and procurement requirements a single person cannot satisfy. Then preview: this guide compares both on cost, speed, quality, risk and communication, and states plainly when *not* to hire a freelancer.

### H2: Should You Hire a Freelance Developer or an Agency?
The extractable answer block. Lead with the one-paragraph verdict, then a decision table:

| Your situation | Better fit |
|---|---|
| Standard website, 1–10 pages | Freelancer |
| Budget under $2,500 | Freelancer |
| You want to talk to the person building it | Freelancer |
| Tight timeline on a defined scope | Freelancer |
| Branding + copy + development + ads together | Agency |
| Enterprise procurement, SLAs, compliance sign-off | Agency |
| Guaranteed cover if one person is unavailable | Agency |
| Ongoing multi-channel marketing retainer | Agency |

### H2: What Does a Freelance Developer Actually Cost vs an Agency?
- My own range for reference: **$500–$2,000** for a complete site, landing page to full business site (link to the cost guide, don't restate the whole breakdown).
- Wider market context: US and Western European agencies commonly quote $5,000–$30,000 for the same brief; small agencies start around $5,000.
- Explain *why* the gap exists: agency price covers project managers, account managers, sales, office overhead and utilisation targets. Not waste — but you pay for it whether your project needs it or not.
- Honest counterpoint (important for trust): the cheapest quote is not automatically the best value. Cross-link the "cheapest option often costs more" argument from the cost guide.

### H2: Can a Freelancer Build a Website as Good as an Agency?
Direct AEO-target section (secondary keyword, 210/mo). Answer yes, with the honest qualifier: on a standard marketing website the deliverable is genuinely comparable, because the work is one designer-developer's craft either way — at an agency it is often a mid-level developer working from a template while the senior name you met is on sales calls. Where an agency reliably wins: work that needs several specialists *simultaneously* (brand identity, copywriting, motion, complex backend, paid media).

### H2: How Fast Is Each One?
- Freelancer: no internal handoffs, no weekly status meeting, decisions in one message. Reference real timelines already published on the site (landing page 3–7 days, 3–5 page site 1–3 weeks).
- Agency: parallel specialists can compress large scopes, but discovery phases, approval chains and account-manager relay add calendar time on small projects.
- The nuance worth stating: agencies win on *throughput* for large scopes, freelancers win on *latency* for small ones.

### H2: What Are the Real Risks of Each?
Two-column honesty section. This is the credibility centre of the article.

**Freelancer risks:** single point of failure (illness, another project, disappearing), capacity ceiling, variable professionalism at the low end. → How to de-risk: written contract, milestone payments, you own the domain and repo from day one, check live references. Link to the buyer's guide.

**Agency risks:** the senior you were sold isn't the one who builds it, account-manager relay slows every question, minimum engagement sizes, small clients get junior staff and back-of-queue priority, higher switching cost.

### H2: When Should You NOT Hire a Freelancer?
The differentiator. Written against my own commercial interest, which is exactly why it earns trust and citations. Be specific:
- You need branding, copywriting, development and paid media delivered together on one timeline.
- Your procurement requires a registered vendor, insurance certificates, or a formal SLA.
- The build genuinely needs several specialists at once (complex backend, native app, data platform).
- You need guaranteed same-day response during business hours with cover for absence.
- Internally you have nobody available to make decisions, so you need a project manager to own the process.

### H2: How Do You Choose Between Them for Your Project?
Practical 5-step close: define the scope in writing → set a real budget range → ask both types for an itemised quote on the same brief → compare what's actually included, not the headline number → check live references either way. Reinforce that the same evaluation checklist applies to both (link to the buyer's guide).

## Competitive Notes

The SERP is dominated by parties with an obvious stake in the answer: marketplaces (Upwork, Arc.dev, Lemon.io) and agencies writing about why agencies are better. Typical length 1,200–2,500 words. Common structure: pros/cons list, cost table, "it depends" conclusion.

What they all cover: cost difference, scalability, communication, reliability, project size.
What they mostly avoid: naming the specific situations where their own model is the wrong choice.

## Differentiation

1. **Written by an actual freelancer, stated openly** — includes a full section on when *not* to hire a freelancer. Nothing else on page one does this credibly.
2. **Real numbers from real projects**, not scraped survey ranges, and linked to live case studies the reader can open.
3. **Risk symmetry** — most competitors treat "freelancer might disappear" as decisive without ever mentioning the agency equivalent (bait-and-switch staffing, junior handoff, account-manager relay).
4. **De-risking checklist** rather than vague reassurance — contract, milestones, domain and repo ownership.
